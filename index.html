01

What we covered

We started on AI and ended up somewhere much more interesting: what Bang has become, and how to say it.

Start with the problem, not the tool

  • You keep AI out of the operational excellence conversation on purpose. Most of the time (your estimate was about 85%) the fix is information not passing from one person to the next.
  • Form logic and a clear, easy process solve that. A little AI on top can come later. Simple is also less likely to break.
  • Your test: is it a shiny thing, or does it move the goal? Pick one goal, whether that's efficiency on the floor, compliance or customer service, and start there.
My parallel: years ago I watched a team build a stack of good automation use cases from the bottom up, then see them die at the executive table because they weren't in the strategy. Strategy and governance first, then the initiatives.

Bang has outgrown its label

  • People know Bang for digital marketing. But if a founder's goal is revenue, marketing is maybe 20% of it. Real results need the whole customer journey: sales process, systems, follow-up.
  • The thirteen leads: "none of them were good." You looked. Every one was aligned. Nobody had followed them up. A business problem, not a marketing one.
  • Clients come in for digital, and the work moves upstream once you start asking questions. It's why some have stayed for twenty years.
  • You say no to work that doesn't fit, including a recent brief whose structure would have burned the team.

Same team, deeper work

  • Five years out: about the same size team, maybe a couple more. No middle managers. Hybrids who learn each other's roles.
  • Meatier, longer engagements with the right clients. More of your time in strategic client conversations, less on the things you shouldn't be doing.
  • Complex clients in new industries keep you learning. That's the joy: connecting dots across them.
The analogy that popped up: a series, not a movie. Long relationships leave room for character development. One of my clients started as one small job and is now two years in.

The thinking is the work

  • We're both building a "brain": structured skills and context that make the work consistent and let anyone step in. The context becomes the IP.
  • Your pitches show it. A full read of the prospect's goals and numbers, built and shared as a live link, rather than "we're Bang and this is what we do".
  • You can see what any other business needs within seconds. Your own is harder, because you're so close to it. That's exactly what the room is for.

03

Your host presentation

The point is connection. The room supports businesses, but it's people who drive them.

It's for the room to get to know you, the journey that got you here, and the business.

I say 10 or 15 minutes; it usually runs 15 to 20, and that's fine. Doing it at your first session works nicely, because everyone else has met over the last few months.

Format is yours. Troy put together a short deck; others haven't used slides at all. A plan on a page or a strategy page is always good to talk to. Photos of the dog and the family are welcome.

The idea I love comes from Leadership WA's "journey of a lifetime": the pivotal points. The moments where this happened and it changed how you think, or you moved to that, and why. As you said, a business only shows the peak of the story. What's under the iceberg is what drives the person.

The more the room understands you, the more it can help, and the easier it is for everyone to open up.

A shape that would work

  1. You: the turning points

    The ones you mentioned are strong. Being held to account for sales results you had no ownership of, and what that made you build. The X Colab chapter, and why you came back to having digital drive the process.

  2. Bang today

    Nearly twenty years. A small, flat, hybrid team. What you do, who you do it for, and a couple of real examples of problems solved.

  3. What's next

    Turning twenty, the move and fit-out, the new website, refreshed brand values. Which leads straight back to your question.

Bookend it with your question. Raise it at the start, then land the presentation on it, so the room is already thinking when issue processing begins.
04

How issue processing works

It's a really powerful process. Here's exactly what to expect, step by step.

  1. You

    State the issue

    Always framed as "How do I...?" because it's about something you want to do.

  2. Linc

    Probing questions

    I ask a few standard ones to build context. Why does this matter to you? What have you tried so far?

  3. The room

    Questions, not answers

    Everyone asks at least one. Not tactical: they try to get below the surface to what's really driving it and how it feels.

  4. The room

    Is it the right question?

    We go around on whether you're asking the right question, and anyone can propose a reframe. You can take it, or tell them to get stuffed and keep your own. Both are fine.

  5. The room

    Advice, as if they were you

    Each member says what they would do in your position. You listen and take notes. No responding while they talk.

  6. You

    Commit

    What, specifically, will you do before we next meet? Two or three ideas out of ten people is a great result. If none land, that's fine too.

  7. Together

    Report back

    We pick it up in your 1:1 and at the next session, so you can see where you got to.

Bring a pen and paper.The advice comes thick and fast. You'll want it written down.

With the fit-out you want people to walk in and think: these are the people I want to work with. The words need to do the same job.

The tension to put to the room

Lead with digital

  • +It's what people know you for, and it opens the door to everything else.
  • +Keeps the creative flair in how you solve problems.
  • −Undersells the upstream work that actually drives the results.

Lead with operational excellence

  • +Describes the real value you deliver.
  • −Can make Bang sound like a systems or IT company.
  • −Risks the wrong projects. You've watched agencies go so far into "intelligent automation" that customers no longer know what they do.
The middle path you floated

Show, don't label. Lead with examples of problems solved, and let the capability speak.

Set it up with stories

Your instinct is right: give the room examples rather than your own labels, so you don't put words in their mouths. Then ask what they'd call it. Candidates from Thursday:

The thirteen "bad" leadsAll aligned, none followed up. The problem was process, not marketing.

Prompts from me
  1. How would your clients explain what you do? Your guess on Thursday: they came in for one thing, we found the real problem, spoke to everyone, and sorted it with the people in mind. Problem solvers, with integrity, who get the foundations right first. Worth asking two or three long-standing clients to write a line before Wednesday.
  2. Who's the buyer? In a mid-sized business, the upstream work is bought by the founder or CEO, not an IT team. The words have to land with them.
  3. The room is your market. Several members run exactly the grounded, practical businesses Bang does its best work with. Their words for what you do are real customer research.
  4. A reference point. Have a look at Marketforce: an advertising agency that built an in-house digital team and moved into transformation work. That team now sits with 303.

You don't have to land the wording now. Just keep it in mind between now and Wednesday.
